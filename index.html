Part A · Graph basics

What is a graph? Vertices, edges and degree

In plain words. A graph is a picture of things and the connections between them. The things are dots, called vertices. The connections are lines, called edges.

Definition. A graph G = (V, E) consists of a non-empty set V of vertices and a set E of edges. Each edge joins two vertices, called its endpoints.

Adjacency and degree. Two vertices u, v are adjacent (neighbours) if {u, v} ∈ E; the edge is then incident with u and v. The degree deg(v) is the number of edges incident with v, a loop counting twice. Handshaking theorem: Σv∈V deg(v) = 2|E|.

Part A · Graph basics

Where graphs began: the seven bridges of Königsberg

The story. In the 1700s the town of Königsberg stood on the river Pregel. The river cut the town into four land areas, joined by seven bridges.

The puzzle. Can you take a walk that crosses every bridge exactly once and brings you back to where you started?

In 1736 Leonhard Euler (say "OY-ler") answered it, and graph theory was born.

Definition. An Euler circuit of G is a simple circuit that contains every edge of G; an Euler path is a simple path that contains every edge. Theorem (Euler, 1736). A connected multigraph with at least two vertices has an Euler circuit if and only if every vertex has even degree.

Part A · Graph basics

Kinds of graph: simple, multigraph, directed

In plain words. Graphs differ in what an edge is allowed to do. Ask three questions: may two vertices be joined more than once? May an edge join a vertex to itself? Do the edges have a direction?

Definition. A simple graph has no loops and no multiple edges. A directed graph (digraph) has edges that are ordered pairs (u, v): each edge starts at u and ends at v.

Definition. A multigraph may have multiple edges: two or more edges joining the same pair of vertices. A pseudograph may also have loops: edges {v, v} joining a vertex to itself. In a digraph, the in-degree deg⁻(v) counts edges ending at v and the out-degree deg⁺(v) counts edges starting at v; Σ deg⁻(v) = Σ deg⁺(v) = |E|.

In a directed graph, (a, b) and (b, a) are two DIFFERENT edges: one goes from a to b, the other from b to a.
Part A · Graph basics

Bipartite graphs: two groups, edges only across

In plain words. Split the vertices into two teams. The graph is bipartite if every edge joins a vertex of one team to a vertex of the other team. No edge stays inside a team.

Definition. A simple graph G is bipartite if its vertex set V can be split into two disjoint sets V₁ and V₂ such that every edge joins a vertex in V₁ and a vertex in V₂.

"Bi" means two, "partite" means parts. Bipartite is a property of the graph, NOT of how it happens to be drawn.
Part A · Graph basics

Paths, circuits and connected graphs

In plain words. A path is a trip through the graph: you move from vertex to vertex, always along an edge. A circuit is a trip that ends where it began. A graph is connected if you can travel from any vertex to any other.

Definition. A path of length n is a sequence of n edges, each starting where the previous one ended. It is a circuit if it begins and ends at the same vertex, and simple if no edge is used twice. A graph is connected if there is a path between every pair of distinct vertices.

Definition. A connected component of a graph is a connected subgraph that is not contained in any larger connected subgraph. A graph is connected exactly when it has one component.

Length counts EDGES, not vertices. The path a, b, c, d has 4 vertices but length 3.
Part B · Planar graphs

What is a planar graph?

In plain words. A graph is planar if you can draw it on a flat sheet so that no two edges cross.

Definition. A graph is called planar if it can be drawn in the plane without edges crossing. Such a drawing is called a planar representation of the graph.

"Can be drawn" is the key phrase. One bad drawing with crossings does NOT prove a graph is non-planar.
Part B · Planar graphs

Untangling the cube Q₃

In plain words. The cube graph Q₃ is usually drawn like a box, and that drawing has crossings. To show that Q₃ is planar we need to find just one drawing of it with no crossings.

How to prove a graph is planar. Show a planar representation: a drawing with the same vertices and the same edges, in which no two edges cross.

Definition. A planar representation of G is a drawing of G in the plane in which edges meet only at common endpoints. G is planar if and only if it has at least one planar representation.

Moving a vertex or stretching an edge never changes the graph. Adding or deleting an edge DOES change it.

Part B · Planar graphs

Regions of a planar drawing

In plain words. Think of the edges as fences on a huge field. The fences cut the field into separate pieces. Each piece is a region. The land outside all the fences is a region too.

Definition. A planar representation of a graph splits the plane into regions, including one unbounded region (the outside). The number of regions is written r.

Definition. The degree of a region R, deg(R), is the number of edges on its boundary; an edge met twice when walking round the boundary is counted twice. Then Σ deg(R) = 2e.

The most common mistake: forgetting the OUTER region. It always counts as one region.
Part B · Planar graphs

Euler's formula: r = e − v + 2

In plain words. For a connected planar graph you never need to count the regions. Count the edges and the vertices, and the number of regions is forced.

Theorem (Euler's formula). Let G be a connected planar simple graph with e edges and v vertices. Let r be the number of regions in a planar representation of G. Then r = e − v + 2.

Two conditions: the graph must be CONNECTED, and r must include the outer region.
Part B · Planar graphs

Checking Euler's formula on many graphs

Do not just believe the formula: test it. Choose a graph, predict the number of regions with r = e − v + 2, then press Count and watch the vertices, edges and regions being counted one by one.

Theorem. Euler's formula v − e + r = 2 holds for every planar representation of a connected planar simple graph. For a planar graph with k connected components: v − e + r = k + 1.

Part B · Planar graphs

Corollary 1: e ≤ 3v − 6, and why K₅ is not planar

In plain words. A planar graph cannot have too many edges. If a graph has more than 3v − 6 edges, there is simply not enough room to draw it without crossings.

Corollary 1. If G is a connected planar simple graph with e edges and v vertices, where v ≥ 3, then e ≤ 3v − 6.

The test works in ONE direction only. If e > 3v − 6 the graph is not planar. If e ≤ 3v − 6 you have learned nothing yet.
Part B · Planar graphs

Corollaries 2 and 3, and why K₃,₃ is not planar

In plain words. K₃,₃ passes the first test (9 ≤ 12), so we need a sharper one. If a graph has no triangles, every region needs at least 4 edges, and the limit on edges becomes smaller.

Corollary 2. A connected planar simple graph has a vertex of degree ≤ 5.  Corollary 3. If a connected planar simple graph has v ≥ 3 and no circuits of length 3, then e ≤ 2v − 4.

Corollary 3 may be used ONLY when the graph has no triangles. K₅ is full of triangles, so use Corollary 1 for K₅.

Part C · Graph coloring

What is a coloring of a graph?

In plain words. Give every vertex a colour. The only rule: two vertices joined by an edge must not have the same colour.

Definition. A coloring of a simple graph is the assignment of a color to each vertex so that no two adjacent vertices are assigned the same color.

The rule is about ADJACENT vertices only. Two vertices that are not joined may share a colour, and usually should.
Part C · Graph coloring

From a map to its dual graph

In plain words. Colouring a map (neighbouring countries get different colours) is the same problem as colouring a graph. We only need to turn the map into a graph.

Dual graph of a map. Each region becomes a vertex. Two vertices are joined by an edge when their regions share a common border.

Two regions that touch only at a single POINT are not neighbours: no edge between them.
Part C · Graph coloring

The dual graph G* of a plane graph: faces become vertices

In plain words. Swap the roles of faces and vertices. Put a dot in every face of G (the outside too), and for every edge of G draw one new edge that crosses it, joining the faces on its two sides.

Definition. Let G be a connected plane graph. Its dual G* has one vertex for each face of G, and for each edge e of G one edge e* that crosses e once and joins the faces on the two sides of e.

G* may have loops and multiple edges even when G is simple: a bridge gives a loop, and two faces that share k edges are joined k times.
Part C · Graph coloring

How to play. Click a region to change its colour (red → blue → green → brown → blank). Two regions that share a border must have different colours.

Goal. Goal: use the smallest possible number of colours. Switch on the dual graph to see the same problem as a graph coloring.

Definition. A map coloring assigns a colour to each region so that two regions with a common border get different colours. It is the same as a coloring of the vertices of the dual graph.

A red dashed line between two regions means they are neighbours with the same colour: fix one of them.
Part C · Graph coloring

The chromatic number χ(G)

In plain words. Colours are expensive: use as few as possible. The smallest number that works is called the chromatic number. χ is the Greek letter "chi" (say "kye").

Definition. The chromatic number of a graph is the least number of colors needed for a coloring of the graph. It is written χ(G).

To prove χ(G) = k you need TWO things: a coloring with k colours, and a reason why k − 1 colours can never work.
Part C · Graph coloring

The four color theorem, and a worked example

A little history. In 1852 Francis Guthrie noticed that 4 colours were enough for a map of English counties and asked whether that is always true. It stayed unproved until 1976, when Appel and Haken proved it with the help of a computer.

The four color theorem. The chromatic number of a planar graph is no greater than four.

The theorem says "at most 4". Many planar graphs need fewer. This one needs only 3.
Part C · Graph coloring

One more edge, one more colour

In plain words. Take the graph G from the last slide and add a single edge joining a and g. Call the new graph H. Does the old coloring still work?

Vertices a and g were both red. Now they are adjacent, so one of them must change. Watch what that forces.

Fact. If H is obtained from G by adding one edge, then χ(G) ≤ χ(H) ≤ χ(G) + 1.

One extra edge can raise the chromatic number. Always recheck the coloring when the graph changes.
Part C · Graph coloring

Chromatic numbers of special graphs

Three families worth remembering. Choose a family and move the slider. The graph is coloured with the smallest possible number of colours.

Definitions. Kn: n vertices, every pair adjacent. Km,n: two parts of m and n vertices, an edge exactly between vertices of different parts. Cn (n ≥ 3): edges v₁v₂, …, vn−1vn, vnv₁. Theorem. χ(Kn) = n; χ(Km,n) = 2; χ(Cn) = 2 for even n and 3 for odd n.

For cycles, check whether n is EVEN or ODD before answering.

Part D · Trees

What is a tree?

In plain words. A tree is a graph in one piece that has no closed loops. Family trees, folder structures on a computer and river systems all have this shape.

Definition. A tree is a connected undirected graph with no simple circuits.

Both conditions are needed. Connected but with a circuit: not a tree. No circuits but in two pieces: not a tree (it is called a forest).
Part D · Trees

Which of these are trees?

Six graphs, one question each: is it a tree? Check two things every time. Is it connected? Does it have a circuit?

Definition. A forest is a graph with no simple circuits; each of its components is a tree. Theorem. An undirected graph is a tree if and only if there is a unique simple path between any two of its vertices.

Part D · Trees

Rooted trees: choose a vertex and let the tree hang

In plain words. Pick any vertex and call it the root. Hold the tree by that vertex and let everything else hang down. Now every edge has a natural direction: away from the root.

Definition. A rooted tree is a tree in which one vertex has been designated as the root and every edge is directed away from the root.

In mathematics trees grow DOWNWARDS: the root is drawn at the top.
Part D · Trees

Family words: parent, child, sibling, ancestor, descendant

In plain words. A rooted tree is described with the words of a family tree. Going up one step you meet the parent. Going down one step you meet the children.

Definition. If v is not the root, its parent is the unique vertex u with an edge from u to v; then v is a child of u. Vertices with the same parent are siblings.

The ancestors of v are the vertices on the path from the root to v, excluding v; the descendants of v are the vertices that have v as an ancestor.

A vertex is NOT its own ancestor or descendant. The root has no parent.
Part D · Trees

Leaves, internal vertices and subtrees

In plain words. A vertex at the end of a branch, with no children, is a leaf. A vertex that has children is internal. Cut off a vertex together with everything below it and you get a subtree.

Definition. A leaf is a vertex with no children. An internal vertex has at least one child. The subtree rooted at a consists of a, its descendants and all edges between them.

The level of v is the length of the unique path from the root to v (the root has level 0). The height of a rooted tree is the largest level of its vertices.

The root counts as an internal vertex (unless the tree has only one vertex).
Part D · Trees

m-ary trees and binary trees

In plain words. Limit how many children a vertex may have. If nobody has more than m children, the tree is m-ary. If every internal vertex has exactly m, it is a full m-ary tree.

Definition. A rooted tree is an m-ary tree if every internal vertex has no more than m children, and a full m-ary tree if every internal vertex has exactly m children. With m = 2 it is a binary tree.

A rooted m-ary tree of height h is balanced if all its leaves are at level h or h − 1.

"Full" is about INTERNAL vertices only. Leaves have 0 children and that is fine.
Part D · Trees

Ordered rooted trees: left child, right child

In plain words. In an ordered tree the children of each vertex have a fixed order, drawn from left to right. In a binary tree the two positions have names: left child and right child.

Definition. An ordered rooted tree is a rooted tree where the children of each internal vertex are ordered. In an ordered binary tree, the tree rooted at the left child of a vertex is its left subtree, and likewise its right subtree.

A vertex may have only a right child, or only a left child. Left and right are different positions.
Part D · Trees

A tree with n vertices has n − 1 edges

In plain words. Count the vertices of any tree, subtract one, and you have the number of edges. It never fails.

Theorem. A tree with n vertices has n − 1 edges.

This only works for trees. A graph with n vertices and n − 1 edges that is NOT connected is not a tree.
Part D · Trees

Counting vertices and leaves in a full m-ary tree

In plain words. In a full m-ary tree, if you know how many internal vertices there are, you know everything: the number of vertices and the number of leaves.

Theorem. A full m-ary tree with i internal vertices has n = mi + 1 vertices and l = (m − 1)i + 1 leaves.

These formulas need a FULL m-ary tree: every internal vertex has exactly m children.
Part D · Trees

Binary search trees: the idea

In plain words. Store items in a binary tree so that smaller items go left and larger items go right. Then finding an item is like looking up a word in a dictionary: each comparison throws away half of what is left.

Definition. A binary search tree is a binary tree in which each vertex has a key, larger than the keys of all vertices in its left subtree and smaller than the keys of all vertices in its right subtree.

The rule must hold at EVERY vertex, not only at the root.
Part D · Trees

Why is “mathematics” the root, and why left or right?

In plain words. Words are inserted in the order they are given, so the first word becomes the root. To choose left or right we compare two words letter by letter, like a dictionary: earlier in the alphabet is smaller and goes left, later is larger and goes right.

Definition (alphabetical order). Word u comes before word w if, at the first position where they differ, the letter of u comes earlier in the alphabet, or if u is a beginning (prefix) of w. In a binary search tree the keys of the left subtree come before the key, and the keys of the right subtree come after it.

The ROOT depends on the insertion order. The LEFT/RIGHT choice depends only on alphabetical order.
Part D · Trees

Building a binary search tree, one word at a time

The rule for inserting. Start at the root. If the new word comes before the word in the vertex (alphabetically), go left; if it comes after, go right. When you reach an empty place, put the word there.

How to use it. Press Step (or →) to see one comparison at a time, or Play to watch.

Algorithm (insertion). To insert key x: start at the root. At a vertex with key k, go to the left child if x < k and to the right child if x > k. At the first empty position, create a new vertex with key x.

Compare words letter by letter, as in a dictionary: "geology" comes after "geography" because l comes after g.
Part D · Trees

Searching a binary search tree

Searching uses the same walk as inserting. Compare with the root: equal means found, smaller means go left, larger means go right. If you fall off the tree, the item is not there.

How to use it. Choose a word and press Step or Play. Count the comparisons.

Algorithm (search). Start at the root. If x equals the key, stop: found. If x is smaller, continue in the left subtree; if larger, in the right subtree. Reaching an empty subtree means x is not in the tree. At most h + 1 comparisons are needed, where h is the height.

The number of comparisons is at most the number of levels of the tree, not the number of words.

Part E · Tree traversal

Tree traversal: visiting every vertex in a fixed order

In plain words. To print, search or copy a tree we must visit every vertex exactly once. A traversal is a rule that tells us in which order. There are three classic rules, and they differ in only one thing: when the root is visited.

Definition. A traversal algorithm is a procedure for systematically visiting every vertex of an ordered rooted tree.

Let T be an ordered rooted tree with root r. If T is only r, every traversal visits r. Otherwise let T₁, T₂, …, Tn be the subtrees at r from left to right; the three traversals are defined recursively from them.

All three orders visit the subtrees from LEFT to RIGHT. Only the position of the root changes.
Part E · Tree traversal

When do we visit a vertex? Walk round the tree

In plain words. Trace a pen round the outside of the tree, starting at the left of the root. You pass every vertex up to three times: on its left (going down), underneath (coming back from its first subtree) and on its right (leaving it for good). Each order writes a vertex down at one of these passes.

Definition. To visit a vertex means to write it down (process it). In the walk round an ordered rooted tree, a vertex is visited in preorder at its 1st pass, in inorder at its 2nd pass (after its first subtree) and in postorder at its 3rd pass (after all its subtrees). A leaf has all three passes in one sweep.

Flags: BLUE on the left = preorder, GREEN underneath = inorder, RED on the right = postorder (the same colours as the video lesson). Write a vertex when the pen touches its flag.
Part E · Tree traversal

Preorder traversal: root first

In plain words. Visit the root first. Then do the whole first (left-most) subtree, then the whole second subtree, and so on. Inside each subtree, follow the same rule again.

Preorder. Visit the root r. Then traverse T₁ in preorder, then T₂ in preorder, …, then Tₙ in preorder.

With the walk (slide 47). Walk the pen round the tree and write each vertex at its 1st pass: the blue flag on its left, when you first arrive.

"Pre" means before: the root comes BEFORE its subtrees.
Part E · Tree traversal

Inorder traversal: root in the middle

In plain words. Do the whole first (left-most) subtree. Then visit the root. Then do the remaining subtrees from left to right. Inside each subtree, follow the same rule again.

Inorder. Traverse T₁ in inorder, then visit the root r, then traverse T₂ in inorder, …, then Tₙ in inorder.

With the walk (slide 47). Write each vertex at its 2nd pass: the green flag underneath, when the pen comes back up from its first subtree. A leaf is written as soon as you reach it.

"In" means in between: the root comes AFTER its first subtree and BEFORE the others.
Part E · Tree traversal

Postorder traversal: root last

In plain words. Do all the subtrees first, from left to right. Visit the root last. Inside each subtree, follow the same rule again.

Postorder. Traverse T₁ in postorder, then T₂ in postorder, …, then Tₙ in postorder, and finally visit the root r.

With the walk (slide 47). Write each vertex at its 3rd pass: the red flag on its right, when the pen leaves it for the last time.

"Post" means after: the root comes AFTER all its subtrees.
Part E · Tree traversal

Definitions (recursive). Preorder: r, then T₁, …, Tn. Inorder: T₁, then r, then T₂, …, Tn. Postorder: T₁, …, Tn, then r. Each subtree is traversed in the same order.

Preorder: root first. Inorder: root in the middle. Postorder: root last.

Part F · Spanning trees

What is a spanning tree?

In plain words. Seven towns are joined by roads. In winter we can keep only a few roads free of snow. Which roads should stay open so that every town can still be reached, using as few roads as possible?

Definition. Let G be a simple graph. A spanning tree of G is a subgraph of G that is a tree containing every vertex of G.

"Spanning" means it reaches every vertex. A tree that misses a vertex is not a spanning tree.
Part F · Spanning trees

Finding a spanning tree by removing edges from circuits

In plain words. A circuit always contains one road too many: if you close one road of a circuit, you can still go round the other way. So keep removing an edge from a circuit until no circuit is left.

Method. While the graph contains a simple circuit, remove one edge of that circuit. The graph stays connected. When no circuit remains, what is left is a spanning tree.

Theorem. A simple graph is connected if and only if it has a spanning tree.

Never remove an edge that is NOT on a circuit: that would cut the graph into two pieces.

Part F · Spanning trees

Depth-first search: go deep, then step back

In plain words. Explore a maze holding a thread. Keep walking to a place you have not seen. When you reach a dead end, follow the thread back one step and look for another unexplored way.

Depth-first search (DFS). Start at a vertex. Repeatedly move to an unvisited neighbour of the current vertex. If there is none, backtrack to the previous vertex. Stop when you are back at the start with nothing left to visit.

The edges used to reach new vertices form a spanning tree, the depth-first search tree. Every other edge joins a vertex to one of its ancestors or descendants in that tree.

Backtracking does not mean starting again. You step back ONE vertex and look for a new way from there.

The edges used to reach new vertices form a spanning tree. DFS trees tend to be tall and thin.
Part F · Spanning trees

Breadth-first search: level by level

In plain words. Drop a stone in a pond: the ripple spreads out evenly. First visit all neighbours of the start. Then all neighbours of those. Then the next ring, and so on.

Breadth-first search (BFS). Put the start vertex in a queue. Repeatedly take the vertex at the front of the queue and add all its unvisited neighbours to the back of the queue.

The vertices are visited in order of their distance (number of edges) from the start. The edges used form the breadth-first search tree.

A queue works like a line of people: first in, first out. That is what keeps the search level by level.

Formally. DFS keeps the vertices still to explore in a stack (last in, first out); BFS keeps them in a queue (first in, first out). In a connected graph both produce a spanning tree.

DFS: one long path, then branches. BFS: a wide fan, level by level.

Minimum spanning trees

In plain words. A company wants to link its five computer centres. Each possible link has a monthly cost. Which links should it lease so that every centre is connected and the total cost is as small as possible?

Definition. A minimum spanning tree in a connected weighted graph is a spanning tree that has the smallest possible sum of weights of its edges.

A weighted graph gives each edge e a number w(e), its weight. The weight of a tree is the sum of the weights of its edges.

A weighted graph has a number (weight) on every edge: a cost, a distance or a time.

Kruskal's algorithm: always take the cheapest safe edge

In plain words. Sort all edges from cheapest to most expensive. Go down the list. Take an edge if it does not close a circuit with the edges already taken; otherwise skip it. Stop when you have n − 1 edges.

Kruskal's algorithm. Repeatedly add an edge of smallest weight that does not form a simple circuit with the edges already chosen, until n − 1 edges have been chosen.

The chosen edges do NOT have to be connected to each other while the algorithm runs. They join up at the end.
Part F · Spanning trees

Kruskal: how to check for a circuit

In plain words. Give every connected piece its own colour. An edge between two different colours is safe: it joins two pieces. An edge between two vertices of the same colour would close a circuit.

Two vertices have the same colour exactly when the chosen edges already contain a path between them. Adding one more edge between them creates a second route, and two routes make a circuit.

Formally. The chosen edges form a forest. Edge {u, v} is accepted if and only if u and v lie in different components of that forest; the two components then merge into one.

After taking an edge, the two pieces merge and must be given ONE colour.

Prim's algorithm: grow one tree

In plain words. Start at any vertex. Look at all edges that lead from the tree built so far to a vertex outside it. Take the cheapest one. Repeat until every vertex is in the tree.

Prim's algorithm. Begin with an edge of smallest weight at the start vertex. Repeatedly add an edge of smallest weight that joins a vertex in the tree to a vertex not yet in the tree, until n − 1 edges have been added.

In Prim the chosen edges ALWAYS form one connected tree. That is the main difference from Kruskal.
